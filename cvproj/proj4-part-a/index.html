A.1 / Photograph pairs

Overlapping views

In my first image set, I took pictures of a church-like structure covered in graffiti and paintings. For my second set, it shows the newly constructed undergraduate building next to a Pimentel lookalike building.

For the third set of pictures, it shows Unit 3 courtyard at nighttime, and I took pictures of Priestley Hall and the Bear Market.

To capture all the images, I kept the camera’s center of projection fixed and rotated it between shots, with the amount of overlap between the pictures as recommended in the spec (mine is around 50–65%). This allows me to capture the transformation for each pair as a homography.

A.2 / Recover homographies

Matched points and projective transforms

Numbered labels identify the same feature in both images. Each point pair contributes two equations for the eight free homography parameters; the overdetermined systems are solved by least squares, with the final matrix entry fixed to 1.

How computeH works

The computeH function takes matching point lists from the two images and returns the 3 × 3 homography H. For a source point (x, y) and its match (x′, y′), the mapping is written as two linear equations in the eight unknown homography parameters:

x a + y b + c − x x′ g − y x′ h = x′\nx d + y e + f − x y′ g − y y′ h = y′$\begin{aligned} x a + y b + c - x x' g - y x' h &= x' \\ x d + y e + f - x y' g - y y' h &= y' \end{aligned}$

The unknown parameter vector is:

h = [a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h]ᵀ$\mathbf{h} = [a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h]^T$

Stacking the two rows from every matched point gives the system below.

Stacking the equations from n point pairs gives a matrix A with 2n rows and 8 columns, and a vector b with 2n entries. Together they form the linear system, where h is the vector of the eight unknowns and b is the vector of target coordinates.

With more than four point pairs, the system is overdetermined, so we solve for the least-squares estimate (thanks EECS 127).

A h = b, A in R^(2n × 8), b in R^(2n)$A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{b}, \qquad A \in \mathbb{R}^{2n \times 8}, \quad \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{2n}$
h = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb$\mathbf{h} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}$

We then place those eight values into:

H = [[a, b, c], [d, e, f], [g, h, 1]]$H = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Nearest neighbor vs. bilinear interpolation

In terms of what it seems like, bilinear produces better results for me. It was smoother and much cleaner.

With nearest neighbor, you could see jagged stuff in the images because every output pixel copies a single source pixel. This is not a problem with bilinear blends because it uses the four neighbors, which removes most of that aliasing. But it is a bit slower and looks a bit softer in comparison.

Nearest neighbor is faster, with one lookup per pixel. Bilinear needs four lookups and a weighted sum per channel. On the poster, nearest neighbor took 7.74 seconds versus 12.42 seconds for bilinear (about 1.6× slower). For the wheelchair sign, nearest neighbor took 10.67 seconds versus 21.85 seconds for bilinear (about 2.0× slower).

A.4 / Blend the images

Three image mosaics

To do the mosaic part of the project, I first computed a homography, H, from the correspondences. One image is the reference and stays unwarped, and then I warp the other into the reference’s frame with bilinear inverse warping. This is because, as I noticed from the previous part, bilinear, even though slower, produced better results for my images, which were without jagged edges.

I then computed the size of the combined canvas from the warped images’ bounding box and the reference image’s extent, and then placed both on it, based on the offsets I got from my computation.

I then blended with an 8-level Laplacian pyramid using the warped images’ mask as the blend mask. The mask is then downsampled along with the images so that the low frequencies blend over a wider part of the images and high frequency over a narrower one, just like we did in Project 2. This helped me smoothen the edges for the image.
